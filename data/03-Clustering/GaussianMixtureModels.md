# Gaussian Mixture Models

A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) assumes the data is generated by a blend of several normal distributions, each representing a cluster. Rather than assigning each point to a single group, it gives the probability of membership in every cluster.

## How It Works

Each cluster is described by a Gaussian distribution with its own mean and covariance, plus a weight giving its overall proportion. The model finds the parameters of these distributions that best explain the observed data, and any point can then be scored against each one.

### Soft Assignments

Unlike k-means, which makes hard assignments, a GMM produces soft assignments: a point might belong sixty percent to one cluster and forty percent to another. This captures uncertainty and overlapping groups more naturally.

## Expectation-Maximisation

GMMs are trained with the expectation-maximisation algorithm. In the expectation step, the model estimates the probability that each point came from each cluster. In the maximisation step, it updates the cluster means, covariances, and weights to fit those probabilities. The two steps alternate until the fit stabilises.

## Covariance Shapes

Because each cluster has its own covariance matrix, GMMs can model elliptical clusters of different sizes and orientations, which is more flexible than the spherical clusters k-means assumes.

## Strengths and Limitations

GMMs provide probabilistic, flexible clustering and can fit overlapping, non-spherical groups. Their drawbacks are sensitivity to initialisation, the need to choose the number of components, and the risk of converging to a poor local solution.

## Use Cases

They are used for speaker identification, anomaly detection, image segmentation, and any task where soft membership and density estimation are valuable.
