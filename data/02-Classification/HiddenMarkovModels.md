# Hidden Markov Models

A hidden Markov model (HMM) describes a sequence of observations that are generated by an underlying chain of hidden states. It is a foundational tool for modelling data where order and context matter.

## How It Works

The model assumes the system moves through a series of hidden states over time, and at each step emits an observable output. We see only the outputs, not the states, and the goal is to infer the hidden sequence that best explains what we observed.

### The Markov Assumption

The next state depends only on the current state, not the full history. This memoryless property keeps the model tractable while still capturing sequential structure.

### Transition and Emission Probabilities

Two sets of probabilities define an HMM. Transition probabilities give the chance of moving from one hidden state to another, and emission probabilities give the chance of producing each observation from a given state.

## Core Problems

Three classic problems are solved with HMMs. Evaluation computes how likely an observed sequence is, using the forward algorithm. Decoding finds the most likely hidden sequence, using the Viterbi algorithm. Learning estimates the probabilities from data, using the Baum-Welch algorithm.

## Strengths and Limitations

HMMs handle variable-length sequences and provide a principled probabilistic framework. Their limitations are the restrictive Markov and independence assumptions, and they have largely been surpassed by recurrent and transformer neural networks for complex sequence tasks.

## Use Cases

HMMs have been used for part-of-speech tagging, speech recognition, gesture recognition, and modelling biological sequences such as genes.
